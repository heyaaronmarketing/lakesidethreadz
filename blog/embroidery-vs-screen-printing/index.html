Embroidery vs Screen Printing: Which One Is Right for Your Logo?

Both put your logo on a shirt. They look different, they cost different, they wear different. Here's how to pick.

Get Your Quote →

The two-second answer

Embroidery for logos on polos, jackets, hats, and anything that needs a premium, raised, business-casual look. Screen printing for t-shirts, hoodies, and runs where cost-per-piece matters more than tactile feel, especially at 25+ pieces. Both are durable. Both look professional. The choice comes down to garment type, quantity, and the feel you want.

Embroidery — what it is and when it wins

Embroidery uses a computerized sewing machine to stitch your logo directly into the fabric using colored thread. The design is raised, you can run a thumb over it and feel the texture, and it looks tailored. Thread colors don't fade because they're actual fiber, not ink. Embroidery is the right call when: the garment is a polo, oxford, jacket, or hat; the logo is 2-4 inches (left chest sized) or smaller; you want the piece to read 'this is uniform-quality' rather than 'this is a promotional tee'; or you're ordering 1-100 pieces where the per-piece setup math works. Embroidery per-piece cost is driven mostly by stitch count (how many stitches are in your logo) and secondarily by thread colors.

Screen printing — what it is and when it wins

Screen printing pushes ink through a mesh screen onto the fabric. Each color in your design requires its own screen, which is why screen-print pricing has a setup fee per color. The ink sits on top of the fabric and gets cured with heat so it bonds permanently. Screen printing wins when: the garment is a t-shirt or hoodie; the design is bold (vs. fine detail); you're printing 25+ pieces of the same thing; you want a soft hand (especially with water-based ink) that feels like the shirt itself; or you need the per-piece cost to be as low as possible on a bulk run.

Side-by-side comparison

Cost per piece at 50 pieces: embroidery $6-15, screen print $4-10. Setup cost: embroidery $0-50 (one-time digitizing), screen print $20-40 PER COLOR. Minimum order: embroidery no minimum, screen print usually 12-24. Best fabrics: embroidery = polos, cotton, canvas, twill, fleece; screen = cotton, 50/50 blends, light polyester. Perceived value: embroidery = premium/uniform, screen = casual/retail-brand. Durability: both excellent — embroidery doesn't fade (thread), screen doesn't crack if properly cured. Max color count: embroidery practically unlimited but expensive at high color counts, screen practically 6 before DTF/DTG makes more sense. Hand feel: embroidery raised and textured, screen soft and flat (water-based) or slightly plastic (plastisol).

When you shouldn't pick either

If your design is full-color photographic (gradients, portraits, photo elements), neither embroidery nor screen printing is the right tool — DTF (direct-to-film) or DTG (direct-to-garment) prints that cleanly in one pass. If you need 1-10 pieces of a full-color design, DTF is almost always cheaper than screen printing's per-color setup fees.

What we recommend at Lakeside

For businesses ordering polos, oxford shirts, hats, jackets, or uniform runs, embroidery is almost always the right call. For t-shirt runs, booster club fundraisers, event tees, or anything where quantity + price matter more than premium feel, screen printing (or DTF for full-color art) is usually it. We do all three in-house, so we'll quote you both when it's a close call.

Frequently asked

Is embroidery more expensive than screen printing?

Per-piece at low quantities (1-25), they're often close or embroidery is cheaper (because screen has per-color setup fees). At 100+ pieces of a simple 1-2 color design, screen printing is cheaper per piece. The exact break-even depends on your design's stitch count and color count.

Does embroidery damage the fabric?

No — embroidery is a sewing process, not a cutting process. The needle passes through the fabric; it doesn't tear. Properly digitized embroidery on quality fabric will outlast the garment.

Can you embroider on a t-shirt?

Yes, but we usually recommend a polo, henley, or ringspun cotton tee for embroidery — the thicker fabric handles the stitch weight better. Light cotton tees can pucker around dense embroidery designs.

Does screen printing crack over time?

Not if it's cured properly. Cracking usually means the print wasn't fully cured during production, or the shirt was washed hot and tumble-dried hot repeatedly. A properly cured screen print survives 50+ washes easily.

Which one lasts longer?

Both last the life of the garment when done well. Embroidery has a slight edge because thread is a physical fiber bonded to the fabric, whereas screen-print ink is a surface layer. But either one typically outlasts the shirt.
